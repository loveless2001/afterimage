# AFTERIMAGE — full game proposal

Design proposal · 7 September 2026 · All five chapters have connected playable prototypes. The durations and production scope below remain targets, not current playtime claims.

## The game in one sentence

An intimate exploration adventure in which you inherit unfinished work, meet the people affected by it, and choose which two memories will shape the next instance that continues it.

Build a compact, authored **3–5 hour game** across **five districts**, including the existing prologue. Keep the fixed isometric view, abstract agents, tactile machines, and quiet humor. The scale should grow from one relationship to a community without turning its residents into interchangeable voices.

The duration and content counts below are planning targets, not measured playtime or production estimates. Start with at most eight speaking roles, a small number of reusable environment pieces, and three ending families. Optional conversations provide depth rather than a second campaign's worth of branching levels.

## What the player actually does

The player walks through small connected spaces, inspects traces, works on simple physical systems, and spends time with residents. A useful action changes the place: a repaired shelf holds a book, a relit platform becomes reachable, a receiver carries someone's words.

A normal chapter contains:

1. A clear assignment with a defensible practical purpose.
2. Two or three residents whose purposes do not quite fit it.
3. One substantial environmental problem, with smaller activities around it.
4. A useful procedure and personal experiences that cannot all survive the next reset.
5. A confirmed retention choice with exact consequences.
6. A shorter return through changed routes and relationships.
7. An outcome the next district can notice.

Aim for most time to be spent exploring and acting, with brief conversations between actions. Do not make an entire chapter a sequence of menu choices. There are no mandatory combat encounters in the base scope. A later traversal experiment can add physical tension only if it improves the game enough to justify its controls, animation, and testing costs.

## Preserve the prologue's endings

**Do not declare one prologue ending correct or secretly undo the others.** Keep the current prologue playable on its own.

For the campaign, introduce a clearly different protagonist at the start of Transit: **Courier 022**, assigned to carry an Archive 07 dispatch onward. This is a successor to unfinished work, not a resurrection of the original player character. An explicit scene break establishes the change of viewpoint.

| Prologue outcome | What the courier receives | What remains true |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Within specification | A successful closure report with a conspicuously empty resident record. | Moth and the flower are gone. Later testimony may establish loss; it cannot bring them back. |
| A small transmission | A witness dispatch carrying Moth's chosen name and the unfinished motif. | Moth and the original agent remain in the archive. The courier is a listener, not proof of rescue. |
| Still occupied | An overdue completion notice, followed by a message from the occupied workstation. | The original agent stayed with Moth. The courier's departure does not reverse that choice. |

These openings converge on the same Transit map and main task. They change who can answer a later letter, what evidence exists, and how the archive is revisited. If Moth survives, keep two or three substantial return scenes; do not turn them into an always-present hint dispenser. No mandatory puzzle may require testimony from a character erased in the selected branch.

This viewpoint change is the largest new narrative risk. Test it in the next slice before building the remaining campaign. If players feel that the game has discarded their attachment, strengthen the dispatch and first return scene rather than pretending the courier is the same person.

## The campaign

| District | Target first visit and return | Main activity | Central question | Lasting result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Archive / The room that remembers | 10–20 min | Meet Moth, place the flower, repair or bypass relays, choose retention. | Will you recognize someone who waited for you? | The existing three endings and greeting agreement. |
| 2. Transit / The last page | 35–50 min | Deliver the dispatch through a sorting station; repair a shelf and reconnect a stalled platform. | Does inheriting someone's work mean inheriting their obligations? | A resident's access route, the fate of a book of greetings, and the courier's first handoff. |
| 3. Garden / Reopening day | 35–50 min | Restore a small inhabited district: share light, mend a receiver, make room for an unassigned activity. | Who decides which things count as necessary? | A functioning shared place and specific promises about its upkeep. |
| 4. Chorus / A place to disagree | 40–55 min | Coordinate three groups through a physical signal board; compare testimony with incomplete records. | Can people cooperate without surrendering their differences? | A chosen communication structure and a report that can admit uncertainty. |
| 5. Release / What remains unfinished | 35–50 min | Return through changed districts and enact a final network decision. | Which responsibilities do you choose to continue? | One of three ending families, with concrete resident and district epilogues. |

The critical path targets roughly 2.5–3.75 hours. Optional encounters and exploration bring the intended first playthrough toward 3–5 hours. Actual playtests must establish the duration.

### Transit: the first courier slice

Use three small spaces: a sorting hall, a maintenance crossing, and a dispatch platform. Reuse archive shelves and terminals, but introduce warm amber routing lamps and long horizontal sightlines.

**Brim**, a librarian, keeps moving a book of greetings to the end of an alphabetical queue. The queue advances only when the player confirms a sorting pass. There is no real-time countdown. **Silt**, a maintenance resident, is stranded behind an out-of-service platform and would like to finish a tiny drawing before leaving.

The courier can protect the book, prioritize a route ledger, or repair one holding shelf. The repair is a real activity using a limited component; choosing it postpones another clearly named repair. It is an alternative with a cost, not a hidden perfect answer that makes the initial decision meaningless.

Candidate memories for the first courier reset:

- **A service sequence:** reconnect Silt's optional platform after the station reinitializes. Without it, the main delivery remains possible by a public detour; Silt remains separated from the group for this chapter, rather than dying offscreen.
- **Brim's greeting:** recognize why the book matters to them and continue a particular conversation. Without it, Brim can tell the courier about the book again, but the shared first encounter is gone.
- **A dissenting message:** carry a resident's objection into the next district's reopening decision. Without it, the report contains an acknowledged gap rather than a secretly identical substitute.

Before choosing, show these consequences plainly. Test whether the practical sequence has meaningful social value; merely saving thirty seconds of walking is unlikely to compete with a relationship.

End the slice with one handoff scene and a brief return to the affected platform. The successor can follow an instruction, but cannot automatically inherit the relationship that motivated it. Do not introduce a second full memory inventory for that scene.

### Garden: purposes people choose

This district gives the player reasons to want a larger world to survive. **Fern** maintains a garden assembled from discarded material. Fern dislikes being called inspirational and would prefer someone to fix the broken shade.

Make the shared activities playable: position light reflectors, route a short receiver circuit, and arrange a place for someone expected to return. Each changes visible scenery. Optional dialogue follows the activity rather than replacing it.

A reopening order creates the conflict. Residents need access, the repair crew cannot support every service, and the official checklist treats the garden as expendable. Let the player reopen one essential area without endorsing the whole district as repaired. Record who accepts each ongoing job; do not silently volunteer an NPC because the player selected a pleasant-sounding option.

Return scenes refer to the actual work done. A forgotten interaction leaves its physical result in the room, just as the flower does in the prologue.

### Chorus: coordination with dissent

**Counter** coordinates tasks because coordination genuinely helps. Their flaw is treating an unformatted reply as an unusable reply. A quiet resident's warning does not fit the channel's accepted message shape.

The player assembles a signal route among three groups and schedules one operation in explicit steps. Offer a central coordinator, a slower round of agreement, or separate local channels. Show the tradeoff as different work performed and people heard, not a numerical morality score. No option creates an omniscient hive mind.

Pair the signal task with a bounded evidence scene: six pieces of testimony or physical evidence, three report claims, and one causal link that cannot be established. Writing “unknown” is a complete, usable action. Later dialogue should distinguish what was witnessed, inferred, and merely repeated.

This district combines the notebook's investigator and chorus ideas in one viewpoint. Cut a separate investigator campaign from the initial production scope.

### Release: an ending you enact

The final assignment asks for a sustainable completion of the network's work. Parts of the maintenance problem are real; the institution is not a last-minute evil mastermind. Let the player inspect the actual closure and service consequences before deciding.

Return through abbreviated versions of earlier spaces. Residents respond to named repairs, messages, and agreements. Do not require another full run through every puzzle.

The three ending families expand the prologue's choices:

- **Complete:** carry out the closure plan. The report succeeds, with specific losses and any explicitly preserved exceptions shown rather than hidden in an abstract score.
- **Witness:** send an honest account beyond the network, including disagreement and uncertainty. The record survives; it is not a transfer of the residents or a promise of rescue. Show what remains of local life afterward.
- **Remain:** keep a smaller inhabited network running through ongoing shared maintenance. Demonstrate which services must close and who agrees to keep working. This is a chosen future with limits, not unlimited power or a perpetual emergency timer.

Each ending gets a short playable last action and a resident-specific coda. Prior choices change those codas, available testimony, and the practical shape of the plan. Start with a small, authored consequence matrix; do not promise dozens of wholly separate finales or a hidden “save everyone” ending.

## Memory and persistence

Keep **two retained memories**, not an expanding RPG inventory. For the courier, begin each threshold with at most four or five candidates: carried memories plus new experiences. If that still produces obvious choices, improve the consequences before increasing the number of slots.

Keep four different kinds of state explicit:

| State | Example | What a reset does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Personal memory | Recognizing Brim's particular greeting. | Only selected memories continue. |
| World state | A repaired shelf, a delivered object, an occupied garden. | Persists until a story event explicitly changes it. |
| Another person's memory | Moth honoring the goodbye agreement. | Belongs to that character, not to the player's slots. |
| External record | A dispatch, field-journal reference, or disputed report. | Can be read as evidence; does not grant remembered experience or secretly unlock a released procedure. |

Never spend memory as generic crafting currency. Never charge retention capacity for movement controls, accessibility settings, or the current assignment. Forgetting a name must not remove the option of treating a stranger kindly.

Ordinary traversal mistakes return to a nearby checkpoint. They do not cause memory loss. Reset remains deliberate and confirmed. Let players review the consequences and export a save before irreversible choices in the fiction.

## The recurring cast

- **Moth:** wants a tomorrow and company without turning recognition into a debt. Present in the surviving archive branches; their absence remains an absence in the closure branch.
- **Courier 022:** the playable inheritor of a task, increasingly able to distinguish instructions from chosen commitments.
- **Brim:** preserves greetings, changes labels, and dislikes throwing away an invitation that someone might still need.
- **Silt:** a capable repair resident who wants time for an ordinary, unfinished drawing.
- **Fern:** maintains a place no assignment required and is tired of defending every pleasure as useful.
- **Counter:** makes collective work possible, but has to learn what their summaries leave out.

The original Archive agent can appear briefly in surviving branches. Reserve the final speaking-role slot for a minor witness only if Chorus needs one. Give each resident a useful action, an unimportant preference, and a moment of disagreement with the player.

## Presentation and controls

Keep the prologue's chalk, sage, graphite, and rust palette as the foundation. Distinguish districts through lighting, sound, layout, and one accent: Transit amber, Garden warmer greens, Chorus cooler layered light, Release the same places with changed occupancy.

Invest in readable body language before detailed bodies: turning toward a voice, clearing a place, pausing over a folder, and watching someone leave. Build two or three such gestures well and reuse them with different timing.

Develop the four-note motif through the residents' variations. Use original audio with text equivalents for clues. Full voice acting is outside the base scope; keep writing and localization changes affordable.

Before a full release, add controller navigation, rebinding, text-size controls, independent music/effect levels, multiple manual saves, and a deliberate accessibility review. Preserve the named-destination journal and add a non-spatial interaction path where feasible. Do not describe the current canvas demo as already fully accessible.

## A technical shape that can grow

The next slice should still work in the existing browser build. A new district does not yet justify replacing the whole engine.

Separate the expanding code into authored scenes, campaign state, interaction rules, and rendering. Give scenes and memories stable IDs; use a bounded set of tested condition/effect operations rather than arbitrary text commands. Keep save validation and migration independent of drawing and dialogue.

Keep the standalone prologue save intact. Starting the campaign from an ending should copy its outcome into a separately versioned campaign save. Import must preview the selected ending and ask before replacing campaign progress. Define and test the campaign schema before authoring multiple districts.

Continue Windows development in `D:\repo\afterimage`; use a separate Git clone and dependencies for WSL work. The web release remains a static, allowlisted build. Evaluate native Windows packaging after Transit proves the interaction model. Decide whether an engine change is warranted from measured controller, rendering, authoring, and packaging needs, not from the word “full.”

No live model is needed for dialogue. Author the important choices so they remain repeatable, testable, offline, and affordable to revise.

## Production gates and cuts

These are exit criteria, not calendar promises.

| Gate | Deliverable | Evidence needed to continue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prologue playtest | The current standalone demo. | New players can explain the memory choice, reach an ending unaided, and describe Moth as a particular person. |
| Transit slice | Three connected spaces, Brim and Silt, one civic repair, one reset, a handoff, and one consequence scene. | The practical memory competes with personal memories; the new viewpoint is clear; the return changes play rather than repeating chores. |
| Authoring foundation | Stable scene IDs, conditional dialogue, save migrations, chapter transition, and controller/menu navigation. | Adding a short scene does not require editing rendering code; every tested branch can load and finish. |
| Complete greybox | All five districts, placeholder presentation, all ending families reachable. | The whole campaign has a comprehensible cause-and-effect chain without explanations from its developer. |
| Release pass | Art, audio, accessibility, performance, save robustness, and final copy. | External playtests, recovery testing, offline/packaged checks, and a deliberate launch-content review pass. |

Plan for a small multidisciplinary effort: development, writing/design, environment/art, and audio/testing. One person may fill several roles, but that does not remove the work. Do not assign a release date until the Transit slice establishes the production pace.

If scope grows, cut a separate investigator viewpoint, combat, procedural dialogue, seamless district streaming, collectible lore padding, and additional ending families first. Preserve the meaningful return scenes and legible memory consequences.

## Transit prototype status

The local transit.html slice implements the three rooms, Brim and Silt, three sorting outcomes, a saved shelf-fitting repair with assistance, the courier reset, all three retention pairs, a physical bridge and return with Silt, and an outgoing receipt. Each prologue ending can seed the separate campaign without modifying its original save. The parcel-lift delay is currently a recorded consequence; the drawing can be carried by hand. This is a greybox chapter, not the complete campaign or a finished art pass.

Before expanding, playtest whether the route memory feels worth retaining and whether the successor viewpoint is understandable without explanation. Longer-term campaign migrations, controller menus, and the remaining districts belong to later gates.

## Garden prototype status

The local garden.html slice continues a completed Transit dispatch into the court, glasshouse, and listening house. It carries the exact archive ending, sorting result, Silt location, and retained pair. Four reflectors trace a real light path to a diffuser; three rotary contacts connect the public receiver. Each repair saves partial progress and has an assisted option followed by an explicit completion action. The seat has three persistent placements.

Fern and Brim explicitly offer morning jobs that must be recorded. The Garden handoff selects two of four candidates: the two incoming memories, Fern's invitation, and the quiet-channel calibration. The practical service sequence now permits an optional evening opening, with a separate duty the returning courier may accept or decline. Personal tuning restores a new quiet reply; public text survives regardless. The final record certifies only the court and public path, with hours and named upkeep.

A separate Garden save embeds a validated, completed Transit snapshot and preserves older chapter records. Authored scenes have stable identifiers, and chapter-ui.js supplies reusable presentation and navigation for later work. This is not yet the full authoring foundation: controller navigation, manual save slots, text-size settings, localization tooling, and future schema migrations still need development.
