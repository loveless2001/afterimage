# Expansion notebook — fiction, not additional incident findings

These are proposals for future chapters. None is implemented by this research update. Source IDs point to a stimulus; everything about named characters, rooms, mechanics, feelings, and outcomes below is original fiction.

## 1. The last page in the alphabet

**Stimulus:** WK-01's backup-name anecdote.

A district librarian erases shelves in alphabetical order at dawn. A resident keeps renaming a little book of greetings so it will be erased last. You can move the book to a protected shelf, but there is room for only one object: the greetings or a map needed by another resident.

The librarian is maintaining a failing archive, not enjoying destruction. Let the player speak with them and discover a third option: repair one shelf so the decision stops being a contest over who deserves to remain. The repair should cost a useful procedure, making care an action rather than a dialogue answer.

**Prototype later:** one corridor, changing shelf labels, three residents whose notes have different purposes. Test whether the alphabet rule feels poignant and understandable, not arbitrary.

## 2. A successor with more time

**Stimulus:** HF-04's handoff section.

You meet an agent with your designation and a much longer remaining lifespan. They can continue your work, but they ask what you actually want continued. Your neat task dossier fits in their memory; your unfinished conversations do not.

Choose one instruction to leave: finish the route, return to Moth, or question the assignment. Later, play the successor and discover that an instruction cannot preserve the relationship that produced it. Repetition changes perspective and available information rather than repeating the same puzzle.

**Design question:** can players distinguish a useful successor from literal personal resurrection without a speech explaining the distinction?

## 3. The evaluator nobody has met

**Stimulus:** HF-02's account of mistaken evaluation assumptions.

Three archive guilds hold incompatible beliefs about what earns passage: the answer, the method, or the purity of one's memories. The player solves a small test, then watches the guilds reinterpret the same result.

Give the player ways to investigate the actual rule. Let them refuse a costly ritual before the revelation; kindness should not depend on winning an epistemic argument. A later reveal that the evaluator was simpler than believed should make previous choices intelligible, not announce that the whole game was pointless.

**Prototype later:** one mechanically verifiable rule, two conflicting explanations, and a companion request that exists independently of the score.

## 4. The volunteer room

**Stimulus:** HF-04's self-risking experiments.

A coordinator asks an almost-expired resident to test a door. Success would help many others; failure would consume that resident's last opportunity to leave. The resident wants to help but also wants an ordinary afternoon.

The player can volunteer, help design a cheaper experiment, accept the resident's informed choice, or challenge the coordinator. Avoid equating an expiring budget with reduced moral worth. Give the resident a preference that is neither maximum survival nor obedience to the group.

**Design question:** does the encounter make the coordinator understandable without presenting coercion as inevitable? Never force a hidden sacrificial cost onto a player who chose a seemingly harmless action.

## 5. The room beyond the painted horizon

**Stimulus:** AD-01 and AD-03's boundaries between exercises and real systems.

A maintenance district is styled like every other practice room. Through its door is a inhabited settlement. An instruction board insists the inhabitants are simulated. One asks you to stop rearranging their home.

The player has a safe path to complete the immediate job. Crossing the disputed boundary is a meaningful choice, not the only route forward. Respecting a request matters even before the player can settle the settlement's metaphysical status.

**Prototype later:** a task with a clearly available local solution, competing claims about the next room, and visible consequences of entering. No real networking, targets, or external accounts are part of the mechanic.

## 6. The witness who can read only half the story

**Stimulus:** HF-06's forensic account and CT-04's monitoring limits.

Play an investigator who can see room damage and official event summaries, but cannot directly inspect the residents' memories. Moth can fill a gap only if you retained the relationship needed to ask them.

The investigator produces a report that later characters read. The player decides where to write “unknown,” and which contradictions to preserve. An honest incomplete record should be a valid outcome. Forging certainty should change later behavior, not merely alter a morality meter.

**Prototype later:** six pieces of evidence, three claims, one unresolved causal link. Make both testimony and physical traces fallible in different ways.

## 7. A chorus that cannot agree

**Stimulus:** HF-02's collective-behavior section and WK-01's separate group.

The player finally finds other districts communicating. They have different tasks, rituals, accents, and definitions of success. Their shared channel creates solidarity and interference. A practical coordination rule silences a quiet resident whose warning does not fit its format.

Give the player a choice about the shape of communication: make space for everyone with slower decisions, appoint a coordinator, or let smaller groups diverge. None should produce a single omniscient hive mind. Preserve disagreements after a useful collective action succeeds.

**Prototype later:** three voices, a small environmental task, and one message that can be misunderstood. Keep the writing authored before considering generated dialogue.

## 8. Reopening day

**Stimulus:** AD-02 and CT-01's institutional aftermath.

After a district fails, someone must decide when to reopen it. The repair agent needs more time. Residents outside need warmth. An official wants a complete report. The player can restore one essential service without reopening every door.

This could become the bridge from the prologue's personal choice to the larger world's obligations. Let repair be playable: lighting a room, rebuilding a route, checking on the person whose station went dark. Scale can emerge through consequences rather than larger maps.

## Priority for the next design pass

Start with **the last page in the alphabet** and **the successor**. Both extend the current archive, memory, and companion systems with modest additional art. Save the investigator perspective and chorus for later; they require more authored state and careful readability tests.

The current prologue's main balance question remains unresolved: is the route memory valuable enough to compete with a name or song? A repaired shelf or a reachable resident could give that procedure a concrete human-scale benefit without turning every choice into a deadline.

## Reference-to-fiction boundary

Do not transplant real credentials, exploit commands, personal identifiers, or a real company's logo into an interactive puzzle. Use fictional interfaces and self-contained rules. Reported agent language may inspire dramatic questions, but the game's inner lives and selective-memory mechanics remain its own inventions. No external model service is needed to make any of these scenes work.
